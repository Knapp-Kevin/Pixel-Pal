# Pixel Pals Art Bible

## Purpose

This document defines the visual language, character construction rules, modular art boundaries, and production standards for Pixel Pals.

The art must support two promises:

> **Where every friendship changes the world.**

> **The first Pal belongs to the player’s story.**

The visual system must be emotionally rich enough to create attachment and structured enough to support many species, regional lineages, inherited traits, clothing choices, ages, expressions, and life events without requiring a unique hand-painted asset for every possible combination.

## Core Visual Promise

Pixel Pals should feel like a warm illustrated world that children want to revisit.

The visual language should communicate:

- warmth;
- playfulness;
- safety;
- personality;
- wonder;
- natural beauty;
- cultural variety;
- emotional honesty;
- gentle mystery;
- accumulated memory.

The game should never feel clinical, disposable, aggressively commercial, visually noisy, or designed primarily to extract attention.

## Canonical Reference Art

The existing storybook character images establish the primary visual direction.

Current visual references include:

- Azuri;
- Iska;
- Kaza;
- Maro;
- Vara;
- Moki;
- Professor Eldan;
- Zeebo;
- Lilu;
- Naila;
- Pohno;
- Reza.

Their shared visual qualities include:

- painterly textures;
- soft natural lighting;
- rounded silhouettes;
- expressive eyes;
- handmade clothing;
- recognizable animal anatomy;
- environment-specific colors and materials;
- personality communicated through posture and expression;
- calm compositions with room for observation.

These images are canonical references for identity and tone. They are not automatically production-ready gameplay assets.

## Two-Layer Visual Model

### Storybook Layer

The storybook layer carries emotional detail.

Use it for:

- character introductions;
- important conversations;
- family portraits;
- journals;
- Great Tree ceremonies;
- adventures;
- major memories;
- remembrance scenes;
- opening and transition art;
- publishing and promotional illustrations.

It may be painterly, richly lit, and compositionally detailed.

### Living-World Layer

The living-world layer supports repeated tablet play.

Use it for:

- village scenes;
- home interiors;
- interactive Pal portraits;
- activity screens;
- relationship views;
- clothing and room customization;
- phenotype previews;
- travel maps;
- daily routines.

It should be simplified, modular where useful, deterministic, readable, and efficient.

The living-world layer must feel like the same world translated into a practical visual grammar, not an unrelated cheaper style.

## The First Pal

The player’s first Pal is not a fixed named story character.

The child names this Pal.

This establishes immediate ownership of the relationship and ensures the opening story belongs partly to the player rather than being entirely inherited from existing canon.

The first Pal should provide meaningful but bounded customization.

### Required first-Pal choices

- name;
- one of a small number of approved species or body-family options;
- a regional or visual starting variation where appropriate;
- base palette or marking choice;
- one clothing item or accessory;
- optional pronouns or equivalent identity presentation, subject to final audience design.

### Traits that should emerge through play

The child should not define every aspect of the first Pal at creation.

The following should be discovered or developed:

- preferences;
- quirks;
- characteristics;
- skills;
- fears;
- friendships;
- ambitions;
- memorable habits.

The first Pal should feel partly chosen and partly met.

That balance matters. Full control produces a character editor. No control produces a mascot assigned by the game. Pixel Pals needs a relationship.

### Naming requirements

Naming should be:

- available before prolonged exposition;
- local-first;
- child-readable;
- easy to revise through a parent-safe or gentle in-world process;
- limited in length;
- protected from accidental confirmation;
- never published publicly by default.

The game may offer suggested names without steering the child toward one canonical answer.

### First-Pal continuity

The first Pal should remain important without becoming mechanically superior.

They may receive:

- a unique first-meeting memory;
- a durable place in the player’s journal;
- a special relationship history with the player;
- recognition in later stories;
- visible aging and life development;
- legacy through family, mentorship, memories, or village history.

They must not receive permanent statistical advantages merely because they were first.

## Visual Priorities

When visual goals conflict, prioritize:

1. Character identity
2. Emotional readability
3. Silhouette clarity
4. Tablet readability
5. World coherence
6. Cultural and environmental specificity
7. Production scalability
8. Decorative detail

A beautiful detail that weakens recognition or usability should be removed.

## Shape Language

Preferred forms:

- rounded silhouettes;
- tapered limbs;
- softened corners;
- natural asymmetry;
- handmade objects;
- irregular but balanced architecture;
- layered foliage;
- curved pathways;
- imperfect household items.

Avoid:

- excessive hard-edged geometry;
- sterile symmetry;
- hyper-polished surfaces;
- dense mechanical ornament;
- sharp aggression unless a specific character or scene requires tension;
- decorative clutter used as a substitute for personality.

## Character Recognition Tests

Every Pal should pass three tests.

### Species Recognition

The viewer should recognize the broad species without relying on clothing or labels.

Recognition comes from:

- head structure;
- muzzle or beak;
- ears;
- limbs;
- paws, hooves, hands, or feet;
- tail;
- body proportion;
- fur, feather, scale, or skin treatment;
- posture.

### Individual Recognition

The viewer should recognize the individual even when clothing changes.

Recognition may come from:

- facial proportion;
- eye shape;
- markings;
- hair or mane shape;
- posture;
- body build;
- characteristic expression;
- one or two signature features.

### Silhouette Recognition

A major Pal should remain recognizable in silhouette at tablet size.

If identity depends entirely on tiny markings or accessories, the design is too fragile.

## Canonical Character Model

Each named or persistent Pal should document:

- species body family;
- regional lineage;
- age stage;
- height and build range;
- head and facial proportions;
- ear, horn, or antler structure;
- tail structure;
- limb and foot structure;
- base palette;
- markings;
- hair, mane, feather, or fur accents;
- signature posture;
- default expression;
- clothing language;
- signature object, if any;
- storybook reference;
- living-world translation;
- immutable identity traits;
- traits allowed to change through age, clothing, environment, or story.

## Species Body Families

The modular system should use controlled body families rather than unrestricted part swapping.

A body family defines:

- torso proportions;
- shoulder and hip anchors;
- limb-length ranges;
- hand, paw, hoof, or foot compatibility;
- head-to-body ratio;
- tail anchor;
- clothing anchor zones;
- pose compatibility;
- age-stage transformations.

Possible production families include:

- small feline;
- large feline;
- canine;
- cervid;
- mustelid;
- ursine;
- primate;
- avian;
- lagomorph.

These are production categories, not claims that species within a family are interchangeable.

## Regional Lineage Design

Regional lineage expands species identity without replacing it.

A regional lineage may influence:

- palette;
- marking probability;
- fur thickness;
- fur texture;
- ear size within safe bounds;
- tail fullness;
- body build within safe bounds;
- inherited environmental adaptations;
- local clothing materials;
- decorative motifs;
- posture tendencies;
- regional symbolism.

A Fox remains recognizable as a Fox across Forest, Snow, Sea, Ember, or Marsh lineages.

Regional lineage must not become one costume applied identically to every species.

## Environment and Appearance

Environment influences appearance through separate channels.

### Inherited adaptation

Generational traits may affect:

- coat density;
- coloration;
- body proportions;
- water resistance;
- heat tolerance;
- ear size;
- foot structure;
- sensory adaptations.

### Developmental expression

A Pal’s lived environment may influence:

- seasonal coat;
- physical conditioning;
- sun exposure;
- grooming;
- posture;
- use of local materials.

### Cultural styling

Clothing, jewelry, grooming, paint, tools, and decoration are learned and chosen. They must never be presented as genetic.

### Spiritual resonance

Subtle visual motifs may suggest an undefined relationship with nature or place. This should remain ambiguous and should not become a standardized religious icon system or measurable spirituality meter.

## Modular Phenotype System

The phenotype system should preserve a rich genome while displaying a controlled number of visual traits.

A Pal normally expresses:

- one primary body family;
- one head and muzzle framework;
- one compatible limb framework;
- one tail framework;
- one base palette;
- one marking system;
- a limited number of secondary inherited traits;
- one optional ancestral accent;
- clothing and accessories independent from genetics.

Most secondary heritage should appear through:

- palette;
- markings;
- ear variation;
- tail variation;
- fur texture;
- controlled body proportions;
- small signature features.

Avoid unrestricted anatomical stacking.

A deer-panda descendant should look like a coherent new individual, not a committee decision with antlers.

## Trait Compatibility

Every visual trait must declare:

- compatible body families;
- required anchor points;
- age-stage compatibility;
- pose compatibility;
- inheritance behavior;
- color-mask behavior;
- fallback asset;
- silhouette impact;
- accessibility risks.

Invalid combinations must resolve safely and deterministically.

## Pose System

The initial pose library should remain small.

Recommended first set:

- neutral standing;
- seated;
- delighted;
- thoughtful;
- concerned;
- sleepy;
- interacting with an object;
- interacting with another Pal.

Storybook art may use unrestricted poses. Modular gameplay portraits should use tested pose families.

## Expression System

Expressions should communicate emotion clearly without exaggerating every feeling into spectacle.

Core expressions may include:

- content;
- delighted;
- curious;
- surprised;
- shy;
- worried;
- frustrated;
- angry;
- sad;
- grieving;
- determined;
- tired;
- playful.

Anger must not automatically make a character look evil. Sadness should not be visually humiliating. Grief may be quiet.

## Clothing

Clothing communicates:

- climate;
- culture;
- profession;
- age;
- personality;
- family history;
- gifts;
- ceremony;
- play.

Clothing should look worn, repaired, inherited, handmade, or personally chosen where appropriate.

Important clothing items may retain memories.

Examples:

- a scarf made by a grandparent;
- a repaired work belt;
- a ceremony ribbon;
- a travel cloak marked by several regions;
- a ridiculous cooking-pot hat adopted through play.

Clothing should fit body-family anchors and should not conceal all species identity.

## Homes and Villages

Homes should visibly reflect their residents.

Art should show:

- preferred activities;
- household composition;
- shared memories;
- local materials;
- repair and age;
- gifts;
- inherited objects;
- cultural exchange;
- environmental adaptation.

Villages should not look like themed amusement parks. They should feel inhabited.

Include:

- storage;
- work areas;
- play areas;
- imperfect repairs;
- seasonal changes;
- children’s spaces;
- gathering areas;
- evidence of everyday life.

## Great Trees

Great Trees are emotional landmarks.

They should feel:

- ancient;
- alive;
- regionally distinct;
- cared for;
- communal;
- mysterious;
- shaped by accumulated history.

Visual history may appear through:

- ribbons;
- lanterns;
- stones;
- carvings;
- blossoms;
- planted gardens;
- benches;
- memorial objects;
- family symbols;
- repaired branches;
- paths worn by generations.

The tree should evolve through player history without becoming a cluttered achievement display.

## Loss and Continuation

Visual treatment of loss should be honest and gentle.

Allowed imagery includes:

- empty familiar places;
- preserved objects;
- gatherings;
- flowers;
- painted stones;
- ribbons;
- seasonal change;
- quiet expressions;
- repaired spaces;
- later joy returning to meaningful locations.

Avoid:

- gore;
- sensational injury;
- manipulative close-ups designed solely to force tears;
- visually freezing the world permanently in grief;
- replacing a deceased Pal with a collectible reward.

Life continues visually. Meals are prepared, children play, weather changes, and homes adapt.

## Humor and Play

The art must make room for harmless absurdity.

Examples:

- mismatched clothes;
- improvised toys;
- failed baking;
- crooked furniture;
- birds stealing hats;
- children overbuilding tree forts;
- mud tracked into a pristine workshop;
- tools repaired far beyond reason;
- playful expressions and visual reactions.

No character should exist only as a joke. Humor reveals personality rather than replacing it.

## UI Visual Language

The interface should feel like part of the world.

Preferred qualities:

- book, journal, map, card, fabric, wood, paper, and painted motifs;
- large touch targets;
- shallow navigation;
- restrained decoration;
- warm surfaces;
- readable typography;
- quiet transitions;
- clear focus states;
- visual confirmation without reward explosions.

Avoid:

- giant flashing quest markers;
- dense icon walls;
- constant particle effects;
- aggressive urgency colors;
- tiny inventory grids;
- excessive badges;
- visual language associated with gambling or loot rarity.

## Motion

Motion should support life and attention, not demand it.

Preferred motion:

- brief expression changes;
- breathing;
- blinking;
- cloth movement;
- foliage;
- water;
- small reactions;
- page turns;
- gentle environmental transitions.

Avoid continuous ornamental movement that drains battery or competes with reading.

Reduced-motion mode must preserve meaning without requiring animation.

## Color

Color should be warm, natural, and regionally specific.

Use contrast intentionally for readability.

Do not rely on color alone to communicate:

- relationship status;
- illness;
- warnings;
- inherited traits;
- selection;
- progress.

Regional palettes should contain internal variety. Not every resident of one village should share the same hue like a municipal branding exercise gone feral.

## Asset Production Requirements

Every production asset must include:

- asset ID;
- source and creator;
- rights and usage status;
- creation date;
- canonical status;
- intended layer;
- supported resolutions;
- compatible anchors or scenes;
- color-mask information;
- accessibility notes;
- replacement or fallback behavior.

## Tablet Performance

Art must be tested against the oldest supported family tablet.

Requirements include:

- responsive image sizing;
- efficient formats such as WebP or AVIF where supported;
- bounded decode cost;
- lazy loading outside the immediate scene;
- controlled memory use;
- limited simultaneous large textures;
- offline caching;
- safe placeholders;
- no dependency on runtime generative image services.

## Runtime Generative AI

Pixel Pals should not rely on runtime generative AI to create canonical character appearances.

Reasons include:

- inconsistency;
- nondeterminism;
- licensing uncertainty;
- child-safety review difficulty;
- inability to guarantee the same Pal appears consistently;
- network dependency;
- style drift.

AI-assisted concept exploration may be used only with clear provenance, human curation, and explicit rights review.

## Initial Art Prototype

The first production-risk prototype should include:

- three species or body families;
- three regional lineages;
- two age stages;
- two poses;
- several palettes;
- several marking sets;
- two clothing sets;
- at least one controlled cross-species descendant;
- the player-named first Pal;
- deterministic rendering from structured phenotype data.

The prototype must test:

- recognition;
- coherence;
- tablet readability;
- asset size;
- missing-layer fallbacks;
- mixed-lineage readability;
- ease of authoring new content.

## Art Review Checklist

Before approving a character or asset, ask:

- Is it recognizably Pixel Pals?
- Is the species readable?
- Is the individual readable?
- Does the silhouette work at tablet size?
- Does the design communicate personality?
- Is the environment or culture expressed without stereotype?
- Can the asset scale into the modular system?
- Does it remain readable with reduced motion?
- Does it have known provenance?
- Does it support fun, relationship, memory, or worldbuilding?
- Would a child remember something specific about it?

## Final Principle

The art should never make a Pal feel like a generated asset slot.

Even when assembled from modular pieces, the result must feel like an individual with a life.

The player names their first Pal because the first relationship should begin with participation and ownership. The world supplies the mystery, personality, history, and change that make that chosen Pal become someone the child could not have fully designed in advance.
